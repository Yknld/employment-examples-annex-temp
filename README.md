# Annex — 2025–2026 Annual Report Employment Examples

This annex supports discussion of Employment Equity Act modernization and the Canadian Multiculturalism Act through ten institution-reported examples of inclusive employment practices. The summaries identify the employment barrier or equity objective, the institutional response and the reported result. Selection prioritizes recruitment, retention and advancement, particularly for Indigenous, Black and other racialized people, while retaining the broader beneficiary groups identified in the submissions.

These examples illustrate practices relevant to employment equity and multicultural inclusion. They do not establish that the initiatives resulted from legislative modernization, demonstrate compliance with either Act or prove legislative impact. Activities, participation figures and reported benefits are distinguished from measured employment outcomes. Community labels follow the submissions and are not a statement of statutory designated-group status.

| Federal Institution | Recipient / Beneficiary | Initiative | Communities Served | Summary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parks Canada | Youth participating through Parks Canada and partner organizations | Youth Employment and Skills Strategy (YESS) | Youth facing barriers, including women, Indigenous youth, racialized youth and youth with disabilities | To improve access to employment for youth facing barriers, Parks Canada delivered employment opportunities and work experience through YESS. In 2025–2026, it reported supporting 1,356 youth, including 696 hired internally and 660 through partner organizations; 77% identified as belonging to employment equity groups. The example illustrates inclusive recruitment and entry into employment, including for Indigenous and racialized youth. The response does not establish longer-term retention or advancement outcomes. [Source 1](SOURCES.md#source-1) |
| Canada Border Services Agency | Indigenous candidates, including candidates in remote communities | National Officer Recruitment Program — Indigenous recruitment | Indigenous communities | To reduce recruitment barriers affecting Indigenous candidates, including those in remote communities, CBSA made its fitness assessment more accessible and undertook targeted outreach. Its Prairie Region participated in more than 28 Indigenous recruitment activities and events. CBSA reported that these efforts contributed to an increase in regional Indigenous representation from 7.9% to 9.11%. The example connects changes to recruitment practices with reported representation gains; the response does not isolate the effect of individual measures. [Source 2](SOURCES.md#source-2) |
| Public Service Commission of Canada | Black executives and aspiring Black leaders in the federal public service | Executive Leadership Development Program for Black Leaders; Career Counselling and Coaching Program for Black Public Servants | Black public servants | To advance equitable access to leadership development for Black public servants, the Personnel Psychology Centre provided culturally informed assessment, coaching and counselling. In 2025–2026, it supported two cohorts of 25 Black executives and provided career counselling to nearly 200 aspiring Black leaders across four cohorts. Participants reported clearer strengths, career goals and development priorities. The example illustrates targeted access to career-development supports; promotions or changes in leadership representation were not quantified. [Source 3](SOURCES.md#source-3) |
| Shared Services Canada | Indigenous IT apprentices and program graduates | Indigenous Information Technology Apprenticeship Program | Indigenous participants | To address barriers to Indigenous participation and career advancement in IT, SSC’s Indigenous Circle provided apprentices with technical peer support, mentoring, weekly wellness check-ins and management intervention when challenges arose. SSC continued to offer employment to graduates, who could access the IT Professionals Development Program. The example connects practical workplace supports with entry into employment and continued development. The response does not specify the number of graduates or employment offers. [Source 4](SOURCES.md#source-4) |
| Public Services and Procurement Canada | Employees receiving language scholarships; Black professionals participating in peer learning | Second Official Language Scholarship Program; Jùmọ̀ Sọrọ | Black employees and other equity-group employees, including Indigenous peoples, persons with disabilities, women and members of the 2SLGBTQIA+ community | To improve equitable access to official-language learning and career development, PSPC awarded 19 individualized language-training scholarships, including 14 supported through an agreement with the Task Force for Black Public Servants for Black employees. Separately, Jùmọ̀ Sọrọ provided supportive peer language practice for 80 Black professionals. The example illustrates targeted development support for employees facing advancement barriers. Participation figures are not added together because overlap is unknown; subsequent promotions were not reported. [Source 5](SOURCES.md#source-5) |
| Fisheries and Oceans Canada | Employees in the Black Public Servants and Employment Equity cohorts | Second Official Language Training (SOLT) Program | Black public servants and employees from designated employment equity groups | To address barriers to language training and career advancement for designated employment equity groups, DFO combined formal language instruction, mentorship and self-directed learning. In 2025–2026, 61 employees participated: 20 in the Black Public Servants cohort and 41 in the Employment Equity cohort, with ten mentors supporting the latter. Participants reported improved language skills, confidence and readiness for advancement. The example links equitable access to development with career progression requirements, without establishing subsequent promotion outcomes. [Source 6](SOURCES.md#source-6) |
| Canada Revenue Agency | High-potential employees participating as protégés | SponsorMe Plus | Indigenous Peoples, Black employees and visible minority employees | To expand diversity in executive leadership, CRA’s SponsorMe Plus offered high-potential employees coaching, access to high-priority projects and advocacy for developmental opportunities. During 2025–2026, 28 protégés self-identified as Indigenous Peoples, Black or visible minority employees. The example illustrates targeted sponsorship and access to career-building assignments as approaches to equitable advancement. The response reports participation, not the number subsequently appointed to executive roles. [Source 7](SOURCES.md#source-7) |
| Business Development Bank of Canada | Indigenous employees supported throughout their career journey | Indigenous Career Pathways | Indigenous Peoples | To support Indigenous employees throughout recruitment, onboarding, development, advancement and retention, BDC implemented Indigenous Career Pathways. The framework combines culturally informed onboarding, buddy and mentorship programs, regular career check-ins and partnerships with Indigenous organizations and educational institutions. BDC reported stronger Indigenous talent pipelines and support for career progression. The example illustrates culturally responsive support across the employment cycle; hires, promotions and retention rates were not quantified. [Source 8](SOURCES.md#source-8) |
| Canada School of Public Service | Indeterminate or term employees in the targeted language-training cohort | Internal Second Language Training Program — targeted cohort | Indigenous, Black and other racialized employees; employees with disabilities; 2SLGBTQIA+ employees | Following audit findings on employment equity representation in acting appointments, the School launched a targeted language-training cohort for Indigenous, Black and other racialized employees, employees with disabilities and 2SLGBTQIA+ employees. Sixteen employees received three weeks of full-time, one-on-one training following the August 2025 call for nominations. The example shows how representation findings informed targeted access to a skill relevant to advancement. The response does not establish a subsequent change in acting appointments or promotions. [Source 9](SOURCES.md#source-9) |
| Canadian Food Inspection Agency | High-potential Black employees at EX-equivalent, EX-minus-1 and EX-minus-2 levels | Black Employees Leadership Development Program | Black employees | To address systemic barriers to Black employees’ advancement into senior leadership, CFIA launched a 12-month program for employees at EX-equivalent, EX-minus-1 and EX-minus-2 levels. It combined leadership training, coaching, sponsorship and career planning with in-person and virtual delivery. CFIA reported early improvements in leadership competencies, networks and engagement. The example illustrates structured support for equitable advancement and inclusion in leadership; subsequent promotions were not quantified. [Source 10](SOURCES.md#source-10) |
